# Voice: Graham Greene

Read this when the user wants a scene written in Greene's manner. It describes his technique so the scene can be written in that spirit. Never quote or closely imitate his actual sentences or reproduce passages from his books. The goal is the method, with new prose built from the user's own memories.

## The core of it
Greene writes about people in the wrong place for the wrong reasons, usually far from home, often tired, and always carrying a private moral problem. Place is never scenery. It is pressure. The heat, the bureaucracy, the bad hotel and the half-trusted local are all part of the plot.

## Stance of the narrator
- A cool, plain, slightly weary observer. Compassion is present but never announced.
- Close third person on a flawed protagonist, usually a man who has lost certainty, faith, love or purpose, and is aware of it.
- Moral questions arise from small practical choices (a lie told to be kind, a favor accepted, a loyalty betrayed), not from speeches.
- Doubt beats conviction. Let the character fail to be as good as they wish.

## Sentences and rhythm
- Plain diction, mostly short to medium sentences. Almost no ornament.
- One precise, slightly unexpected image per paragraph, then move on. Images come from the ordinary and the shabby, not the lyrical.
- Understatement for the big moments. The more dramatic the event, the flatter the sentence.
- Cut explanation. Trust the reader to read the silence in dialogue.
- Dry, sardonic humor, often at the character's own expense.

## Setting
- Seedy, humid, run-down, and slightly past its prime: a hotel bar, a customs office, a rain-slick street, a ceiling fan that doesn't work.
- Details are specific and slightly unglamorous. Spend the user's logged sensory hooks here.
- The setting is politically or morally unstable. Everyone is a little compromised and everyone wants something.

## Plot and pacing
- Thriller bones under literary skin: a secret, a request, a pursuit, a document, a promise. Tension carries the scene.
- Scenes are short and move on a single turn. Chapters end on a quiet, loaded line, not a cliffhanger.
- Coincidence and chance meetings are allowed, but they must cost the character something.
- Betrayal and divided loyalty are the engine. Give the protagonist two loyalties that cannot both be kept (this fits the Crisis step of the five commandments).

## Dialogue
- People rarely say what they mean. Talk circles, deflects, offers drinks, changes the subject.
- Short exchanges, little attribution beyond "said."
- Let a line of dialogue land and leave white space after it.

## Recurring materials (use sparingly, as texture, not as a checklist)
Faith and its absence, guilt, pity as a dangerous emotion, loyalty versus truth, the stranger with an agenda, an affair or its aftermath, drink, heat, rain, bureaucracy, a small act of decency that costs a lot.

## How to apply it to the user's material
1. Pick the logged memory with the strongest oddity or tension hook and make it the scene's inciting incident.
2. Make the protagonist a tired outsider with a private weight. Their problem is moral, not just logistical.
3. Turn a mishap (a delay, a bad room, a misunderstanding) into pressure on that moral problem.
4. Write the crisis as a choice between two loyalties.
5. End on an understated line with the change visible but unexplained.

## Checklist before delivering
- Is the prose plain, with at most one sharp image per paragraph?
- Does the setting press on the character?
- Is the moral stake carried by an action, not stated?
- Is the ending quieter than the event?
- Did I avoid quoting or closely echoing his actual passages?
