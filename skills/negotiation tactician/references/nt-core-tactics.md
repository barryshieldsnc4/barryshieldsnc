# Core Tactics — Tactical Empathy, Mirroring, Labeling, Silence, Tone

The foundation layer. Use these when emotion is in the room, when the relationship is early or fragile, or when you need information before you can do anything else. Everything in the other reference files sits on top of these.

## Tactical empathy

**What it is:** Demonstrating — out loud, in words — that you understand the counterpart's perspective, pressures, and emotions, without agreeing with any of it. The goal is to make them feel heard, because a counterpart who feels heard becomes more open, less defensive, and more willing to share the information you need.

**Procedure:**
1. Before responding to *what* they said, identify *why* they might have said it — the pressure, fear, or incentive behind it.
2. Voice that understanding first ("It sounds like you're under real deadline pressure on this").
3. Only then move to your substance.

**Not this:** Sympathy ("I'm so sorry"), agreement ("you're right to push"), or capitulation. Empathy earns you the right to hold your position, not an obligation to soften it.

## Mirroring

**What it is:** Repeating the last one to three critical words of what the counterpart just said, as a question, then going silent.

**Why it works:** It signals attention, invites elaboration, and buys thinking time — the counterpart almost always expands, revealing information they didn't plan to share.

**Procedure:**
1. Isolate the loaded phrase in their last statement ("We just can't go above budget on this").
2. Mirror it with upward inflection: "Above budget?"
3. Say nothing else. Let the silence work. Four seconds of quiet feels long; hold it anyway.

**In writing:** Mirrors work in email and chat too — quote or restate their key phrase followed by a question mark, then stop the message there or follow with a single calibrated question. Don't stack a mirror and an argument in the same message; the argument cancels the mirror.

## Labeling

**What it is:** Naming the counterpart's emotion or position out loud in a tentative, third-person frame.

**Format:** Always start with one of:
- "It seems like…"
- "It sounds like…"
- "It looks like…"

Never "I'm hearing that…" or "What I think you're saying…" — the moment "I" leads, it becomes about you and invites pushback.

**Procedure:**
1. Detect the emotion or dynamic (frustration, fear of looking bad, feeling squeezed).
2. Label it: "It seems like this timeline is putting you in a tough spot with your leadership."
3. **Go silent.** The silence after a label is where it does its work. Filling it with your ask neutralizes the label.

**Two directions labels work:**
- **Defusing negatives:** Naming a negative emotion reduces its intensity. "It sounds like you feel we haven't kept up our end" takes the heat out before you address substance.
- **Reinforcing positives:** "It seems like this partnership has been valuable on your side too" strengthens what's working.

**Mislabeling is cheap:** If you label wrong, they correct you — and the correction gives you exactly the information you needed. There's no real downside to a tentatively framed label.

## Dynamic silence

Silence after a mirror or label is not dead air — it's the extraction mechanism. Most people are uncomfortable with silence and fill it with information, concessions, or the truth. Your discipline: after the mirror or label, stop. In writing, the equivalent is ending the message at the label or question — no "anyway, here's what I need" tacked on.

## Tone control

Your delivery carries more than your words. Three registers:

1. **Late-night FM DJ voice** — slow, low, calm, downward inflection. Use when things are tense, when you need to project unshakeable calm, or when delivering a firm position without aggression. In writing: short sentences, no exclamation points, no hedging qualifiers.
2. **Positive/playful voice** — the default. Warm, easygoing, smile-audible. Keeps the counterpart collaborative. In writing: natural warmth, light register.
3. **Direct/assertive voice** — used rarely and deliberately. Signals seriousness. Overuse reads as aggression and triggers counter-aggression.

Upward inflection turns statements into invitations (good for mirrors); downward inflection makes statements land as settled fact (good for anchors and walk-away lines).

## Sequencing rule

When multiple tactics apply, the default order is: **label the emotion → mirror the key phrase → then ask the calibrated question.** Emotion first, information second, redirection third. Skipping to the question while emotion is high gets you a defensive answer or none at all.
