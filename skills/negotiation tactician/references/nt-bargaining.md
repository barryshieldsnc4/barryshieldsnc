# Bargaining — the Ackerman Model, Anchoring, Ranges, Fairness, Deadlines, Non-Monetary Terms

Use this file whenever numbers or concrete terms are on the table: price, salary, scope, dates, contract terms. These mechanics assume the core tactics are already in play — never open with a number before empathy has been established.

## Anchoring

- **Whoever anchors first frames the range.** If you're well-informed about market value, anchoring first with an aggressive-but-defensible number pulls the whole negotiation toward you.
- **If you're less informed, let them anchor** — but armor yourself first: their number will exert gravitational pull, so write down your target *before* the conversation and treat their anchor as information, not as the range.
- **Deflecting their extreme anchor:** Don't counter immediately (countering legitimizes it). Instead: label it ("It seems like you're aiming high to leave room"), or ask the calibrated question **"What's making this the right number?"**, or pivot to non-monetary terms before returning to price.
- **The range trick:** When you must name a number in a soft context (e.g., salary expectations), give a bolstering range where your actual target is the *bottom*: "At firms like X, this role runs $140–160K." The counterpart hears the bottom as your floor and rarely offers below it — and a range feels less confrontational than a point demand.

## Odd, precise numbers

$103,500 lands as calculated and credible; $100,000 lands as an opening bid to be shaved. Precise, non-round numbers signal you've done the math and dampen the counterpart's urge to counter aggressively. Use them on final offers especially.

## The Ackerman model

A systematic concession sequence that feels like real movement while conceding a controlled amount. For any target price:

1. **Set your target** (the number you actually want).
2. **First offer: 65% of target** (if buying; invert the logic if selling — first ask at ~154% of target).
3. **Three raises: to 85%, then 95%, then 100%** of target.
4. **Before each raise**, deploy empathy and calibrated questions ("How am I supposed to do that?") — make them work for every step; never concede in response to nothing.
5. **Decreasing increments** — each step is smaller than the last, signaling you're being squeezed to your true limit.
6. **Final number: precise and odd** (e.g., $37,850, not $38,000).
7. **At the final number, attach a non-monetary item** you plausibly don't care much about — it signals you've hit bone and gives them a face-saving win.

**Worked example (buying, target $80,000):**
- Open: $52,000 → "How am I supposed to get to your number?" → $68,000 → label their frustration, extract movement → $76,000 → final: **$79,850 + you'll handle the transition support yourselves.**

## Fairness — the F-word of negotiation

"Fair" is the most powerful and most abused word at the table. Three moves:

1. **Defensive:** When they say "we just want what's fair," it's usually pressure. Respond with a label and a question: "It seems like fairness matters a lot here — what would fair look like specifically?" Make them define it; vague fairness claims collapse under specificity.
2. **Pre-emptive:** Early in a hard negotiation, offer: "I want you to feel this is fair the whole way through — if at any point you think I'm being unfair, stop me and we'll address it." This defuses the fairness weapon before it's drawn.
3. **Never accuse them of unfairness** — it triggers identity-level defensiveness. Label the *situation* instead.

## Deadlines and time pressure

- **Deadlines are usually softer than they sound.** Most are self-imposed or organizational fictions. Test with calibrated questions: "What happens on your end if we don't close by the 30th?"
- **Their deadline is your leverage.** Counterparts concede fastest near their own deadlines. If you know their quarter-end, fiscal pressure, or internal commitment, pace the negotiation toward it.
- **Never let a deadline force a bad deal.** The discipline phrase, internal or external: **no deal is better than a bad deal.** The walk-away line from the prep sheet exists precisely for the moment a deadline is pressing.
- **Revealing your own deadline** can be tactically sound — it prevents last-second impasses and signals honesty — but only reveal real ones.

## Non-monetary terms

Price is one dimension; deals are made in the others. Before bargaining, list what's cheap for you but valuable to them, and vice versa: timing, scope, exclusivity, references/case-study rights, payment schedule, support terms, title, start date, review timing. Trade across dimensions instead of splitting the difference on price — **splitting the difference is the lazy outcome this whole method exists to avoid** (half-measures produce outcomes nobody actually wanted, like one shoe each).

## Handling hardball

- **Threats/ultimatums:** Absorb with a calibrated question ("How am I supposed to move forward under those terms?"). Never counter-threaten in kind; never comply immediately.
- **"Take it or leave it":** Usually a test. Label it, go silent, or restate your position in late-night-DJ register and let them return to the table.
- **When you must say no without the word:** Escalating ladder — (1) "How am I supposed to do that?" → (2) "Your offer is very generous, but it just doesn't work for me" → (3) "I'm sorry, I can't do that" → (4) flat "no" only as the final rung.
