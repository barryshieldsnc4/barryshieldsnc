# Calibrated Questions, "No"-Oriented Framing, and the Two Kinds of "Right"

Use this file when the user needs to redirect a negotiation, extract information, get a counterpart to solve the user's problem, or secure genuine commitment rather than polite agreement.

## Calibrated questions

**What they are:** Open-ended questions starting with "how" or "what" that give the counterpart the feeling of control while making them work on your problem. They cannot be answered with "yes" or "no," and they carry no accusation.

**Banned openers:** "Why" (it reads as an accusation and triggers defensiveness — the only safe use is when defensiveness would work in your favor: "Why would you ever change from your current vendor?" invites them to argue *for* you). Also avoid questions answerable with a bare "yes" — they produce commitment-free agreement.

**The workhorses:**
- **"How am I supposed to do that?"** — the polite way to say no without saying no. Forces them to consider your constraints and often produces a concession. Deliver it as a genuine request for help, not sarcasm.
- **"What's the biggest challenge you're facing here?"** — opens the information channel; surfaces the real constraint behind their position.
- **"How does this fit with what your team is trying to accomplish?"** — reveals the hidden stakeholders and criteria.
- **"What happens if we do nothing?"** — makes the cost of no-deal vivid on *their* side.
- **"How can we solve this problem?"** — enrolls them as a collaborator instead of an adversary.
- **"What's making this the right number?"** — challenges an anchor without confrontation.

**Procedure:** One question per message or per conversational beat. Ask, then silence. Stacked questions let them answer the easy one and skip the hard one.

**Absorbing aggression:** A calibrated question is also a shock absorber. When they lob a threat or ultimatum, "How am I supposed to do that?" declines the ultimatum without defiance and puts the problem back in their lap.

## "No"-oriented framing

**The insight:** "Yes" is often worthless — people say it to escape pressure (counterfeit yes) or to be polite. "No" makes people feel safe and in control, and a counterpart who has said "no" comfortably is *more* likely to engage honestly afterward.

**Procedure:** Frame your asks so the productive answer is "no":
- Instead of "Do you have a few minutes to talk?" → **"Is now a bad time to talk?"**
- Instead of "Do you agree with this approach?" → **"Would it be ridiculous to consider a different structure?"**
- Instead of "Can we get this done by Friday?" → **"Is Friday completely off the table?"**
- Dead thread revival, the single most reliable one-line email: **"Have you given up on this project?"** — a "no" answer re-opens the conversation almost every time.

**Three kinds of "yes"** — learn to tell them apart:
1. **Counterfeit** — said to escape; watch for it after pressure.
2. **Confirmation** — a factual answer, no commitment implied.
3. **Commitment** — the real thing; it comes with specifics about *how* they'll follow through. Only this one counts.

## "That's right" vs. "you're right"

**"That's right"** is the breakthrough marker. It happens when your summary of *their* position, feelings, and stakes is so accurate they can't help confirming it. It means they feel fully understood — and from that moment they become dramatically more movable.

**How to earn it:** Deliver a **summary** = label + paraphrase. Restate their world back to them — their pressures, their logic, their emotions — better than they said it themselves. Then stop. If they say "that's right," you've unlocked the negotiation.

**"You're right"** is the opposite: a dismissal. It means "please stop talking." If you hear it (or its email cousin, "fair point, anyway…"), you have not been heard — back up, label, and summarize again.

## Guaranteeing execution — the Rule of Three

Agreement isn't execution. Before treating a deal as done, get the counterpart to affirm it three times in the same conversation, in different forms:
1. The initial agreement or commitment "yes."
2. A label or summary of it ("So it sounds like this approach works on your end").
3. A calibrated question about implementation ("What does the next step look like on your side?" / "How will we know we're on track?").

Someone who can't walk through the *how* three different ways hasn't actually committed. Also watch pronouns: counterparts who say "I'll make it happen" are often not the real decision-maker; the ones who say "we" and "they" usually sit closer to the actual power — map the ones they mention.
