# Counterpart Reading — Negotiator Types, Black Swans, and the 7-38-55 Signal Check

Use this file to type the counterpart, adjust pacing, and hunt for the unknowns that change everything. Reading the counterpart correctly determines *which* tactics land and *at what speed*.

## The three negotiator types

Everyone defaults to one of three styles. Identify the counterpart's type from their behavior, then adjust — and know your own type, because your defaults read differently to each of them.

### Analyst
- **Signals:** Methodical, prepared, unhurried; long silences (they're thinking, not pressuring); dislikes surprises; corresponds in precise, complete messages; skeptical of charm.
- **What they value:** Time to process, data, accuracy, reciprocity of information.
- **How to work them:** Slow down. Lead with data and precedent. Give them material *before* meetings. Never spring numbers live — send them ahead. Their silence is not an opening to fill; filling it costs you credibility. Reciprocate every disclosure they make, or they'll stop disclosing.
- **Danger:** Pushing pace or improvising reads as unserious and shuts them down for good.

### Accommodator
- **Signals:** Warm, relationship-forward, generous with time, quick to say "yes," conversation drifts sociable; conflict-avoidant.
- **What they value:** The relationship, being liked, smooth interactions.
- **How to work them:** Be sociable — the relationship talk *is* the negotiation channel. But treat their "yes" as counterfeit until proven: pin every agreement to specifics with calibrated implementation questions ("What does the next step look like on your side?"). Their biggest tell is what they *don't* say — objections surface as silence or delay, never as words.
- **Danger:** Mistaking friendliness for agreement. Accommodators will let a deal die quietly rather than voice a problem.

### Assertive
- **Signals:** Direct, time-driven, wants to win the exchange, talks more than listens, interrupts, respects strength.
- **What they value:** Being heard first, efficiency, control, respect.
- **How to work them:** Let them talk themselves out *before* making your points — they cannot hear you until they feel heard. Mirrors and labels work brilliantly on them. Be direct in return; hedging reads as weakness. Expect an aggressive anchor and don't take it personally — it's an opening bid, not an insult.
- **Danger:** Matching their aggression escalates; absorbing it silently teaches them it works. Absorb with calibrated questions instead.

**Type-mismatch warning:** Silence means three different things — thinking (Analyst), hidden objection (Accommodator), impatience (Assertive). Diagnose type *before* interpreting behavior.

## Black swans

**What they are:** The unknown unknowns — pieces of information that, once discovered, change the entire negotiation. Every counterpart has around three. A confusing counterpart is not irrational; they are acting on constraints, fears, or incentives you haven't found yet.

**Where they hide:**
- **Constraints they're embarrassed by** — a budget already spent, an authority they don't actually have, a deadline imposed from above.
- **Other deals in their world** — the alternative they're comparing you to, the internal project competing for the same money.
- **Personal stakes** — a promotion riding on this, a prior failure they can't repeat, a boss they fear.
- **Their worldview/"religion"** — the organizational or personal belief system that makes their position feel righteous to them. Deals move when framed inside their worldview, not yours.

**How to hunt them:**
1. Treat every "irrational" behavior as evidence of a hidden constraint and ask *what would make this behavior rational?*
2. Use face time or live calls — black swans surface in unguarded moments, off-agenda remarks, and hallway conversations, almost never in the formal exchange.
3. Probe with calibrated questions aimed at their world: "What's driving the timing on your end?" "How does this decision get made over there?"
4. Watch for the mismatch between what they say and what they do — the gap is where the swan lives.

**Leverage taxonomy** (what a black swan gives you):
- **Positive leverage** — you have something they want.
- **Negative leverage** — you can credibly withhold or cause loss (use with labels, never explicit threats: "It seems like you're concerned about how a delay would land internally").
- **Normative leverage** — their own stated standards and rules; showing the gap between their principles and their position moves them without confrontation.

## The 7-38-55 signal check

In emotionally loaded communication, the words themselves carry a small fraction of the message; tone carries far more, and body language the most. Treat the exact percentages as a heuristic, not physics — the operational rules:

1. **When words and delivery disagree, believe the delivery.** A "yes" with a flat tone and crossed arms is a "no" you haven't heard yet. Label the gap: "I heard yes, but it seemed like something about this still doesn't sit right."
2. **Prefer richer channels for hard moments.** Escalate email → call → face-to-face as stakes rise; you cannot read what you cannot see or hear.
3. **In text-only negotiation**, the tells shift to: response latency, message length changes, pronoun shifts (I → we → they), formality shifts, and what got ignored in your last message. A suddenly short, formal reply from a previously warm counterpart is a tone signal — treat it exactly like a change in body language.
