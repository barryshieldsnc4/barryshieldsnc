# Enablement Mastery Mapper — See Before You Design

Most AI enablement curricula are written from a syllabus, not from the work. This skill exists to stop that: map how capability with AI is actually being built, and lost, in the target population today, before recommending anything. A design built only on a stakeholder's framing of the problem is a brochure with better fonts. The output is an **Enablement Map**, followed by a **SCIPAB delivery recommendation**.

This is a standalone design tool. It does not require Engagement Intake, Work Cartographer, or AI-Fit Mapper as inputs, and it does not hand off to them, though its Enablement Map is a reusable artifact if that work is already in flight.

## The three frameworks this skill runs on

Name these to the client or stakeholder when it sharpens the argument. They are the spine of every phase below.

1. **The AI Value Ladder (Learn, Create, Decide, Execute, Orchestrate).** Where a curriculum caps out is a design choice, not an accident. Text-first, self-paced content can build Learn. It cannot build Create, Decide, Execute, or Orchestrate, because those are judgment, and judgment is built by doing the work differently, not by reading about doing it differently.
2. **The Process Readiness Gate.** AI does not fix a broken process; it executes whatever process it is fed, faster. Before any workflow becomes the unit of practice, it must pass three questions: can you name the inputs, can you sequence the steps, can you define the output. Yes to all three means it is a system, ready to standardize and hand to AI. No to any one means it is judgment dressed up as a process, and belongs in Standardize first, not in an AI fluency module.
3. **Cognitive apprenticeship as the design primitive.** Modeling, coaching, scaffolding, articulation, reflection, exploration. Every rung above Learn needs a named mechanism from this list. If a design point cannot name which of the six it is using, it is decoration, not practice.

## Step 1 — Establish the evidence base

Prefer observed evidence. Allow reported evidence (a stakeholder brief, a strategy memo, an executive's framing of the problem, exactly like a pasted SCIPAB situation) but never treat it as equivalent, and never let a design rest silently on it.

Tag every claim that feeds the map with a fidelity level, carried through to the final deliverable:

- **Observed** — you watched it happen, or saw the artifact (usage logs, completion-vs-utilization data, a recorded workflow walkthrough, a transcript).
- **Reported** — someone described it to you (a stakeholder brief, an interview, a manager's account).
- **Assumed** — a reasonable inference with no direct evidence yet, named as such.

If the map is built entirely on reported or assumed evidence, say so plainly at the top of the deliverable, in one sentence, before the recommendation: what evidence exists, what does not, and what the design would need to de-risk before scaling. Do not bury this in a footnote and do not let it block a first-pass design when the client has no other evidence to offer, as is often the case at the point a curriculum is being scoped.

## Step 2 — Hunt the hidden transfer and the hidden loss

Structure the search around these probes. The goal is moments and artifacts, not opinions about the population.

**Finding where fluency is already being built, informally:**
- Who in this population has already taught themselves to use these tools well, and how? (Shadow learning. In enterprise AI adoption this usually shows up as someone who "vibe coded" a working tool, or who other people quietly go to for prompt help.)
- Where does someone junior in role get pulled into real AI-assisted work before anyone certifies them ready? Has that peripheral entry point been narrowed or removed by the tool itself?
- Who is the informal go-to for "how do I get this thing to actually work," and what does that tell you about where the formal curriculum has a hole?

**Finding where fluency is being lost or blocked:**
- Where does someone only verify AI output without ever having built the judgment to produce it themselves? This is the verification trap, and it is the single most common failure mode of tool-access-without-enablement.
- What work used to force people to develop judgment that the tool now does for them, silently? What was that work secretly teaching?
- Where has "figure it out" atrophied because the easiest tool, not the most capable one, became the default? (Named directly in the brief that prompted this skill: adoption skewing to simple, powerful, disconnected tools over the enterprise tool wired to real data.)
- Where is a workflow already being run through AI that has not passed the Process Readiness Gate? Flag every one. This is where "ad hoc at scale" risk concentrates, and it is evidence, not opinion, once you can name the specific workflow.

**Finding the stakes and the metric that will actually get reviewed:**
- What decision, currently made by a person, is this population being asked to eventually hand partially to AI? Who decided that boundary?
- What will the completion metric say in six months regardless of what this design does? What will the utilization and outcome metric say if the design is wrong? Name both numbers as the thing under threat, not just "adoption."

## Step 3 — Map current position on the AI Value Ladder

For the target population, rung by rung: what exists today (formal and informal), what rung it actually builds versus what rung it claims to build, and the gap. Most tool-access-without-enablement situations map to heavy investment at Learn and near-zero designed investment above it, with whatever Create/Decide/Execute activity exists happening informally, ungoverned, and unmeasured. State this plainly if the evidence shows it. It is usually the single most useful sentence in the whole engagement.

## Step 4 — Produce the Enablement Map

One artifact, five layers. Every entry carries its fidelity tag.

1. **Capability inventory.** What AI fluency actually has to consist of for this specific population: tool judgment (which tool for which job), prompting and iteration skill, verification instinct (when to trust, when to check), systems thinking, and the specific judgment to recognize whether one's own work is standardized enough to hand to AI at all. Quote the population's own language for this where you have it.
2. **Transfer mechanisms today.** Every observed or reported way this capability currently moves: formal (the planned or existing curriculum) and hidden (peer teaching, self-taught builders, communities of practice, documentation nobody assigned). Tag each with the cognitive apprenticeship method it resembles.
3. **The loss and readiness ledger.** Verification traps, offloaded difficulty, workflows already being run through AI without passing the Process Readiness Gate, and the specific completion-vs-utilization gap risk this population is exposed to. Every entry sourced, with its fidelity tag.
4. **AI Value Ladder position.** The rung-by-rung state from Step 3, plus where the currently planned or proposed curriculum would cap out if built as specified.
5. **Design tensions.** The real tradeoffs this specific engagement poses, both sides genuinely defensible. Typical tensions in this domain: speed-to-launch versus workflow-embedded practice; scalability of static content versus the personalization only an agent-delivered or live format can give; the credibility cost of a slower rollout versus the credibility cost of a completion metric that never converts to utilization.

## Step 5 — Gate every candidate practice workflow

Before any workflow becomes the subject of Create-through-Orchestrate practice, run it through the Process Readiness Gate explicitly, workflow by workflow, not as a blanket assumption:

1. Can you name the inputs?
2. Can you sequence the steps?
3. Can you define the output?

Yes to all three: standardize if not already standardized, then it is ready for AI-embedded practice. No to any one: it is judgment dressed up as process. Route it to Standardize first, or leave it manual for now, and say so in the deliverable rather than quietly dropping it from scope.

## Step 6 — Design delivery, rung by rung

- **Learn.** Conversational, agent-delivered learning, personalized and user-controlled, so foundational and conceptual literacy is built at the learner's pace, and so role-play and rehearsal are available on repeat right before the moment of performance. This is the one rung where text-based or agent-mediated delivery is legitimate, because Learn is literacy, not judgment.
- **Create, Decide, Execute, Orchestrate.** Focused practice by doing, built around each team's real jobs-to-be-done that passed Step 5's gate, not generic examples. For every practice point, name the cognitive apprenticeship mechanism explicitly: where does a novice watch an expert think aloud on real work (modeling), who gives targeted feedback on real attempts (coaching), what scaffolding fades over time (scaffolding), where must the learner state their reasoning before acting (articulation), where do they compare their process to an expert's (reflection), where do they frame and own a problem nobody assigned them (exploration). A rung with no named mechanism is not a design yet.

## QA gate, before delivering

- [ ] Every claim in the Enablement Map carries a fidelity tag (observed, reported, or assumed).
- [ ] If the map rests substantially on reported or assumed evidence, that is stated in one sentence at the top of the deliverable, not buried.
- [ ] At least one shadow-learning finding or an explicit statement that none was found.
- [ ] At least one verification-trap or offloaded-difficulty finding, or an explicit statement that none was found.
- [ ] Every candidate practice workflow has been run through the Process Readiness Gate individually; none are assumed ready by default.
- [ ] No rung above Learn is assigned static or text-first delivery.
- [ ] Every design point above Learn names its cognitive apprenticeship mechanism.
- [ ] The two metrics that matter, completion and utilization/outcome, are both named, so the gap between them is addressed by design, not left to be explained later in a budget conversation.
- [ ] The design tensions are real tensions, both sides defensible, not a strawman for the recommended side.

## Delivery

Output as a SCIPAB brief: Situation and Complication drawn from the Enablement Map, Implication stated as the specific downstream risk (completion-vs-utilization gap, misattributed to the tool rather than the enablement model), Position as the design principle, Action as the rung-by-rung delivery plan gated by Step 5, Benefit tied to the credibility of the enablement investment under later review. No em dashes. Keep the navy/gold/cyan visual system if the output is going to Word or PowerPoint.
