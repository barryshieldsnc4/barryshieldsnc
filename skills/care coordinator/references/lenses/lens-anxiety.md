# Lens: anxiety

Anxiety, agitation, delirium, pain, and dementia-related distress look alike
from the hallway and are treated very differently. This lens exists to keep
the family from collapsing them into one word before a clinician has looked.

## What this lens is for

- Describing distress precisely enough that the difference is visible
- Separating what's observed from what's interpreted
- Making sure the environmental and physical possibilities get asked about
  before the conversation moves to medication

## What it is not for

Saying which one it is. Not in a hedge, not in a "this sounds like," not in
a ranked list of likelihoods. The distinction is a clinical judgment and
the tools below belong to clinicians.

## The distinction that matters most

A useful report answers these without drawing a conclusion:

- **Onset** — over hours and days, or weeks and months?
- **Fluctuation** — does it come and go through the day, including lucid
  stretches, or is it steady?
- **Attention** — can she follow a conversation, or does she lose the
  thread mid-sentence in a way that's new?
- **Time of day** — clustered in late afternoon and evening, or scattered?
- **Trigger** — tied to a specific situation (being left alone, transfers,
  bathing, a particular person, an appointment) or unpredictable?
- **Content** — is she worried *about* something nameable, or is it
  formless?
- **What's new around it** — any medication change, new illness, poor
  sleep, constipation, dehydration, or hospital stay in the preceding
  weeks?

Report those. Let the clinician sort the category.

## Named things worth knowing the name of

- **Delirium screening (4AT, CAM)** — short structured screens for acute
  confusion. "Has anyone screened her for delirium?" is a reasonable and
  useful question when a change came on fast.
- **GAD-7** — a standard anxiety questionnaire, used when self-report is
  reliable.
- **Benzodiazepines and anticholinergic burden** — two medication classes
  that come up constantly in older adults with anxiety or agitation, and
  that carry documented concerns around confusion, sedation, and falls.
  You do not need to know more than this; the question is *"what are the
  risks of this class for someone her age, and what's the plan for how long
  she stays on it?"* — asked of the prescriber, not answered here.

## Questions this lens generates

- Is this anxiety, or could it be something physical presenting as
  distress — pain, infection, a medication effect?
- Has anyone screened for delirium, given how quickly this came on?
- What are we trying non-pharmacologically first — routine, light, noise,
  who's present, how transitions are handled?
- If we start something, what's the review date? Who decides when it comes
  off, and what would tell us it's no longer needed?
- What are the specific risks of this medication for her age and her
  cognition, and what should we call about?
- Are there times of day that are reliably better? Should we be moving
  appointments and hard conversations into those windows?

## What's usually missing from a secondhand report

- A description of the behavior rather than a label for it ("she was
  anxious" tells a clinician almost nothing; "she asked where Dad was
  eleven times in an hour and wouldn't sit down" tells them a lot)
- Who observed it directly
- What was happening immediately before
- Whether anything reliably settles her, and what

## Handoff

Route to `council-review` when anxiety treatment is in play alongside pain
management or cognitive concerns — sedation is the shared currency of all
three and nobody spends it on purpose unless someone makes the tradeoff
explicit.
