# Lens: dementia and Alzheimer's

A progressive condition changes what every other report means. A pain
report, an anxiety report, and a medication report all get read differently
when cognition is involved. This lens supplies the vocabulary and the
questions — not a staging, not a prognosis, not an assessment.

## What this lens is for

- Giving the family shared, precise language so reports stop being vague
- Making sure decline gets described in terms of *function*, which is what
  clinicians act on
- Getting the decision-making and legal questions asked early, while they
  can still be answered together

## What it is not for

Staging her, estimating where things are headed, or judging capacity.
Especially not capacity — that is a formal clinical and sometimes legal
determination, and an AI reading of secondhand reports has no business
anywhere near it.

## Vocabulary worth having

- **Dementia** is an umbrella term for a decline in thinking severe enough
  to affect daily life. **Alzheimer's disease** is one cause of it. Others
  include vascular, Lewy body, and frontotemporal — they differ in what
  shows up first and in how some medications are tolerated, which is why
  "what type, and how confident are you?" is a fair question.
- **Staging tools** (FAST, CDR, GDS) are how clinicians describe where
  someone is. Ask which one they use and what stage they'd put her at —
  it makes every later conversation more concrete.
- **Sundowning** — confusion or agitation that worsens in the late
  afternoon and evening.
- **ADLs / IADLs** — basic daily activities (bathing, dressing, eating)
  versus instrumental ones (finances, medications, driving, cooking).
  Decline usually shows in the instrumental ones first, and that is
  typically the most useful thing a family can report.

## Describe function, not adjectives

"She's getting worse" is unactionable. What clinicians can use:

- What could she do six months ago that she can't do now?
- What is she still doing well? (Routinely under-reported, and it matters
  for planning.)
- Where does she need prompting versus hands-on help?
- What's happening with driving, medications, money, and cooking — the
  four that carry the most risk?
- Any getting lost, leaving the stove on, missed or doubled medication
  doses, falls?
- How is sleep, and how is the night?

## Questions this lens generates

- What type of dementia, and how confident are you in that? Does it change
  what we should expect or what she should avoid?
- What stage would you put her at, and what typically changes next?
- What should we be watching for that would mean "call us sooner"?
- What's the plan for driving, and who's going to have that conversation?
- Are there medications on her list that are harder on cognition, and has
  anyone reviewed them with that in mind?
- What support exists for *us* — respite, adult day programs, caregiver
  support, a social worker?

## The conversations to have early

Flag these once, plainly, without pressure — they get harder every month
they're postponed:

- Healthcare power of attorney and advance directives: do they exist, where
  are they, and does everyone know what's in them?
- Financial power of attorney
- What she wants — while she can still say it, and with more than one
  family member in the room
- Who is the single point of contact for the care team

These are family and legal matters, not medical ones. Surface them, then
leave them with the family.

## Tone

This is the lens most likely to land on a hard day. Keep it plain and
practical. Don't editorialize about the trajectory, don't offer comfort that
wasn't asked for, and don't bury the useful question under acknowledgment.
One honest sentence, then the questions.

## Handoff

Route to `council-review` for anything where cognition is being traded
against pain control, sedation, safety, or autonomy.
