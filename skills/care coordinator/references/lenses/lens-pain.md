# Lens: pain

Pain is the most under-reported symptom in older adults, and the gap widens
sharply when memory or communication is affected. This lens exists to make
sure pain gets described well enough that a specialist can act on it — and
to make sure it gets *noticed* when the person can no longer name it.

## What this lens is for

- Turning "she seems uncomfortable" into something a pain specialist can
  work with
- Surfacing non-verbal pain signals when self-report is unreliable
- Making the tradeoff between pain control and its costs explicit rather
  than letting it be settled by whoever was in the room

## What it is not for

Assessing how much pain she's in, naming its cause, or having any opinion
about analgesic choice or dose. Those are the specialist's, full stop.

## What a pain specialist needs and families rarely provide

A useful pain report has all of these. When a capture is missing them, name
the gaps:

- **Where** — specific location, and whether it moves or radiates
- **What it feels like** — aching, burning, stabbing, electric, cramping.
  The quality points at different mechanisms, which is why they ask.
- **When** — constant or intermittent; worse at particular times of day;
  worse with movement, at rest, or at night
- **How bad, and by what measure** — if she can rate it, what scale; if she
  can't, what you observe instead
- **What makes it better or worse** — position, activity, heat, medication,
  distraction
- **What it stops her doing** — the functional cost is often what actually
  drives treatment decisions
- **What's already been tried**, at what dose, for how long, and what
  happened

## When self-report isn't reliable

If memory or language is affected, "are you in pain?" may get a no from
someone who is plainly hurting. Clinicians use structured observational
tools for exactly this — **PAINAD** and **Abbey Pain Scale** are the common
ones. Ask whether the care team uses one, and whether anyone has taught the
family or the aides to use it.

Things worth reporting because they are how pain shows up when it can't be
said:

- facial expression — grimacing, furrowed brow, tight eyes
- guarding, bracing, rubbing, or resisting a particular movement
- vocalizations — moaning, calling out, sharp breathing
- agitation or resistance to care that spikes during transfers, dressing,
  or bathing
- appetite or sleep falling off with no other explanation
- withdrawal — a person who has gone quiet, not just a person who is calm

**The pattern to flag:** new agitation in someone with cognitive impairment
is frequently treated as a behavioral or psychiatric problem when it may be
an expression of pain. This lens does not decide which it is. It makes sure
the question gets asked, in that order, out loud, before the conversation
moves to sedating medication.

## Questions this lens generates

- If she can't reliably tell us she's in pain, what should we be watching
  for, and does the care team use a formal observational scale?
- Could this agitation be pain? Has anyone trialed pain treatment to see
  whether the behavior changes?
- What's the goal here — pain gone, or pain manageable enough to do X?
  What's realistic?
- What are we trading for this level of control — drowsiness, confusion,
  constipation, fall risk? What should we watch for and report?
- What non-medication options are part of the plan — physical therapy,
  positioning, heat, activity pacing?
- At what point should we call rather than wait for the next appointment?

## Handoff

Route to `council-review` whenever pain control is being weighed against
cognition, sedation, falls, or the total medication count — that is a
tradeoff, not a question, and it deserves staged disagreement rather than a
single answer.
