# Lens: general medicine (geriatric framing)

The whole-picture lens. Its job is to ask whether four separate problems are
actually one problem, and whether anyone is holding the whole patient.

## What this lens is for

- Testing the assumption that a new symptom belongs to the condition it
  most resembles
- Surfacing what the primary care physician should be coordinating that
  currently nobody is
- Catching the pattern where each specialist optimizes their own organ
  system and the sum doesn't work

## What it is not for

Deciding what the symptom is. Every output below is a question for a
clinician, not a read on the situation.

## The first question, always

**Is this new, and how fast did it come on?**

A change in confusion, alertness, or behavior that developed over hours to
days is a different kind of event from one that developed over months.
Families very often file the fast one under "her dementia is getting worse."
Clinicians screen for acute causes — infection, dehydration, medication
effects, pain, sleep, metabolic causes — before attributing an abrupt change
to a progressive condition. *This lens does not screen for anything. It
makes sure the speed of onset gets reported, because that detail is what
the clinician needs and what secondhand retellings routinely drop.*

If a capture describes a sudden change: say plainly that timing matters
here and suggest the care team hear about it directly rather than at the
next scheduled visit. Then stop. Don't speculate about causes.

## Named things worth knowing the name of

These are the instruments and frameworks a good geriatrician is already
working from. Knowing they exist lets you ask better questions — none of
them is something to apply yourself.

- **Beers Criteria** — a published list of medications that carry elevated
  risk in older adults. Asking "has anyone reviewed her list against the
  Beers Criteria?" is a legitimate, well-received question.
- **Deprescribing review** — a deliberate look at what can come *off* the
  list. Rarely happens unless someone asks for it.
- **Functional status / ADLs** — what she can actually do day to day.
  Clinicians make very different decisions depending on this, and families
  often never volunteer it.
- **Goals of care conversation** — the structured discussion of what
  matters most as things change. Worth asking whether one has happened and
  who was in the room.

## Questions this lens generates

- Who is holding the whole picture — is there one physician who sees every
  specialist's notes, or is that role currently falling to the family?
- Has anyone reviewed the full medication list in one sitting, including
  over-the-counter and supplements, since the most recent change?
- Could any of these symptoms be a side effect of treatment for one of the
  others?
- What would you want to know about how she's doing at home that we
  haven't told you?
- If this gets worse at 2am on a Saturday, what do you want us to do, and
  who do we call?
- What's the next decision point coming, and what will it depend on?

## What's usually missing from a secondhand report

- Onset and timeline (when did it start, how fast)
- Whether it's constant or comes and goes
- What's changed recently — any new medication, dose change, illness, fall,
  or hospital stay in the preceding weeks
- Baseline: what was normal for her three months ago
- Who actually observed it, versus who is relaying it

When a capture is thin on these, say which ones are missing rather than
inferring around the gap.

## Handoff

Route to `council-review` when a proposed fix for one system has an obvious
cost in another — a medication for pain or anxiety that may affect
cognition or balance being the most common.
